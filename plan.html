How this works, in plain wordsWhat a Monte Carlo simulation is, why do this exercise, and how to read the results.

What is a Monte Carlo simulation?

A way to explore an uncertain future by playing it out many times. The name comes from the casino in Monaco. Like a roll of the dice, each run draws a random but realistic sequence of market years, some good and some bad, and applies your income and spending to them. This page runs 2,000 of these futures for your numbers, then counts how often the money lasts.

Why not just use an average return?

Markets never deliver the average smoothly. Two people with the same average return can end up in very different places if one meets the bad years right after stopping work. One average hides that; thousands of futures show it. Press “Show 5 sample futures” on the savings chart to see individual paths.

How do I read the savings chart?

The solid line is the typical future: half the futures end above it and half below. The darker band holds the middle half of futures, the light band 8 in 10. The bottom of the light band is a bad-luck case, though not the worst one.

What does a “90% chance” mean?

In 9 of 10 simulated futures the savings never ran out before the end of the plan. It is not a promise. The result is only as good as the assumptions, and real people adapt, for example by spending less after a bad year, which the model does not do. Many planners aim for roughly 80–90% and then review the plan every year.

Which numbers matter most?

Usually how much you spend and when you stop working. How the savings are invested matters too, and market returns matter a lot, but you can't choose them. “What moves the needle” tests each change on the same 2,000 futures, so the differences come from the change, not from luck.

What does the model leave out?

Detailed tax rules, spending cuts after bad years, health shocks, inheritances, separation, and the chance that the future is harsher than history. Use it to compare choices and to prepare good questions, not to predict an exact number.

        What we assumed

          Six ideas that matter for any retirement

          Think in today's euros

          Every amount here is shown in today's money unless you switch to future euros. At 2.5% inflation, what costs €100 today costs about €210 in 30 years.

          The gap is the job of your savings

          Spending minus pensions, rents and other income is the yearly gap. Divide it by your savings to get a withdrawal rate. Starting around 3% to 3.5% has held up through most of history for long retirements; above 5%, the outcome depends mostly on luck.

          Bad years early hurt most

          A crash in the first years after you stop working does more damage than the same crash later, because you sell investments at low prices to pay the bills. A cash buffer of two or three years softens this.

          Fees and taxes compound

          Losing 1% a year to fees and taxes leaves you with about a quarter less after 30 years. It is one of the few things you fully control.

          Safety has a price

          Deposits and guaranteed funds rarely beat inflation by much. Stocks swing, sometimes by a third in a year, but over decades they have grown faster. The mix is a choice between smoother and bigger.

          Not all wealth is spendable

          Homes, locked pension plans and start-up stakes count in net worth but cannot pay this month's bills. The simulation counts only money you can use, unless you choose to sell.

          Questions to ask an AI assistant

          Assistants such as Claude or Grok are good at explaining terms, checking assumptions and walking through what-ifs step by step. Copy a question, or copy your whole plan and paste it in. Replace anything in [brackets] with your own details.

              Getting good answers

              • Say which country you live in and which accounts you hold; the rules differ a lot.
              • Give amounts per year, after tax, in today's money, the way this page does.
              • Ask the assistant to list its assumptions, show its working and say what it is unsure about.
              • Check tax and legal points with a qualified professional before acting.
              • Never paste account numbers, ID numbers or passwords.

              Method. 2,000 futures, one year at a time. Each year draws random returns for every kind of investment (moving together the way markets tend to), adds income, pays spending and records whether money is left. Savings are rebalanced yearly; royalties drift and jump at random; each angel cheque gets a random outcome and exit year; inflation is fixed at your chosen rate.

              Example numbers. The households are illustrations built from reasonable assumptions, not anyone's real accounts. Type your own figures; they stay in this browser only.
